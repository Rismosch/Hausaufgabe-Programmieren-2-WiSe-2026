\section*{Evaluation Contexts}
%===============================================================================

The upper rules are computation rules, the lower congruence rules.

Computation rules:

\[
\begin{array}{rlr}
v := & n & Values \\
v_1 \oplus v_2 \rightarrow & & (v_1 \oplus v_2) \\
\texttt{ifz}(0; t_1, t_2) \rightarrow & & t_1 \\
\texttt{ifz}(v; t_2, t_2) \rightarrow & & t_2\ \text{if}\ n \ne 0
\end{array}
\]

Congruence rules:

\[
\begin{array}{ccc}
\inferrule{t_1 \rightarrow t'_1}{t_1 \oplus t_2 \rightarrow t'_1 \oplus t_2} &
\inferrule{t_2 \rightarrow t'_2}{v \oplus t_2 \rightarrow v \oplus t'_2} &
\inferrule{t_1 \rightarrow t'_1}{\texttt{ifz}(t_1; t_2, t_3) \rightarrow \texttt{ifz}(t'_1; t_2, t_3)}
\end{array}
\]

Congruence rules re-formulated using \textit{evaluation contexts}:

\[
\begin{array}{ll}
E ::= & \\
& [\ ] \\
& E \oplus t \\
& v \oplus E \\
& \texttt{ifz}(E; t_2, t_3)
\end{array}
\]

\subsection*{Normal Forms}
%===============================================================================

A term which is in normal form and is a value is quite simple. Any term in the form of $t = v$ solves the task, e.g: $t_1 = 42$. However, there exists no term which is in normal form and is not a value.

\begin{lemma}\label{normal-1}
Every term $t$, which is not a value, is not in normal form.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof} By contraposition: Every term $t$, which is in normal form, is a value.\\
Due to it being in normal form, no congruence rules and almost no computation rules apply. The only rule left is $v := n$.
\end{proof}

\begin{lemma}\label{normal-2}
Every term $t$, which is not in normal form, is not a value.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}

The grammer of the language only allows for three types of terms: values; concatenation between terms using binary operations; or using \texttt{ifz}, which consumes 3, possibly different terms. \\

To not be in normal form, a term has to be found which cannot be evaluated. A value would fit this requirement, but the question also asks for a term that is not a value. Therefore, a concatenation between binary operations or usage of \texttt{ifz} has to be found. \\

Let's first look at the binary operations. Three cases: \\

\textbf{(i)} $t_1 = v_1, t_2 = v_2$: \\

Computation rule 2 applies.\\

\textbf{(ii)} $t_1 = v_1, t_2 = t_2$: \\

$t_2$ is either concatenated with a binary operator, or uses \texttt{ifz}, both of which can be evaluated. Congruence rule 2 applies. \\

\textbf{(iii)} $t_1 = t_1, t_2 = v_2$ \lor $t_1 = t_1, t_2 = t_2$:\\

Analog to (ii), $t_1$ is either concatenated with a binary operator, or uses \texttt{ifz}. Congruence rule 1 applies. \\

\hrule
%-------------------------------------------------------------------------------
\vspace{1em}

Secondly, let's look at \texttt{ifz}. Three cases: \\

\textbf{(iv)} $n = 0$: \\

Computation rule 3 applies. \\

\textbf{(v)} $n != 0$: \\

Computation rule 4 applies. \\

\textbf{(vi)} $n != t$ for $t \to t'$: \\

Congruence rule 3 applies. \\

\hrule
%-------------------------------------------------------------------------------
\vspace{1em}

Of course, (ii), (iii) and (vi) are recursive. However, the \texttt{size} of a term never grows. It only shrinks, or stays the same when $t = v$, as described in Section \ref{induction-on-derivation-trees}. Therefore, for each term, a computation step exists, meaning there exists no normal form for a term that is not a value. \\

\end{proof}

Because Lemma \ref{normal-1} and \ref{normal-2} hold, there cannot exists a term, which is in normal form and is not a value. \hfill \(\blacksquare\) \\

\hrule
%-------------------------------------------------------------------------------
\vspace{1em}

To create a term that is stuck, we would need to build an infinite term, like $t = t_1 \oplus t_2 \oplus ...$, or introduce a syntax error, for example $t = foo$, $t = + 4\ 3$, or $t = \texttt{ifz}(1; 2)$, but the grammar of our language prohibits terms like these, which means these terms are not part of the language.

\subsection*{Induction on Derivation Trees} \label{induction-on-derivation-trees}
%===============================================================================

\begin{lemma}
$\texttt{size}(t) \ge \texttt{size}(t') \mid t \rightarrow t'$
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof} Three cases.

\textbf{(i)} $t = v$

\begin{align}
\texttt{size}(t) = \texttt{size}(v) = 1 = \texttt{size(t')}
\end{align}

\textbf{(ii)} $t = t_1 \oplus t_2 \mid t_1 \rightarrow^* v_1$

\begin{align}
\texttt{size}(t_1 \oplus t_2) 
&= \texttt{size}(t_1) + \texttt{size}(t_2) + 1 \label{eq:case-2-1}\\
&\ge \texttt{size}(v_1) + \texttt{size}(t_2) + 1 \label{eq:case-2-2}\\
&= \texttt{size}(t')\\
\Leftrightarrow \texttt{size}(t_1) &\ge \texttt{size}(v_1) \\
\texttt{size}(t_1) &\ge 1
\end{align}

Analog for $t = t_1 \oplus t_2, t_2 \rightarrow^* v_2$ \\

\textbf{(iii)} $t = \texttt{ifz}(t_1; t_2, t_3) \mid t_1 \rightarrow^* v_1$

\begin{align}
\texttt{size}(\texttt{ifz}(t_1; t_2, t_3))
&= \texttt{size}(t_1) + \texttt{size}(t_2) + \texttt{size}(t_3) + 1 \\
&\ge \texttt{size}(v_1) + \texttt{size}(t_2) + \texttt{size}(t_3) + 1\\
&= \texttt{size}(t')\\
\Leftrightarrow \texttt{size}(t_1) &\ge \texttt{size}(v_1) \\
\texttt{size}(t_1) &\ge 1
\end{align}

\end{proof}
